02 / DESIGN VISION

Challenge the player without relying on timers

When analyzing other existing cooking games, I noticed that many relied heavily on pressure stemming from time to challenge the player. I wanted to explore a different approach: a cooking game where the challenge came primarily from making good decisions rather than making them quickly.

The Main Design Challenge

How can a cooking game challenge players through preparation, decision-making and precision without relying too much on time pressure?

My approach was to build interconnected systems around interpreting customer requests, selecting appropriate recipes, preparing ingredients and evaluating the finished dish.

03 / GAMEPLAY SYSTEMS

Designing the cooking experience

SYSTEM 01 · ORDERING

Turn dialogue into a decision

Rather than having customers request a specific dish, I designed the ordering system around interpreting their dialogue and determining what they want to eat.

My intent

Encourage players to pay attention to customers and think critically about which dish would best satisfy their requests.

Player-facing behaviour

The player picks up the chef's hat, approaches the ordering window and interacts with the customer. The customer then describes what they want, providing clues about suitable ingredients.

EXAMPLE CUSTOMER REQUEST
“I want something with tomatoes.”

The player needs to interpret this clue and determine which available recipe would best fit the request.

SYSTEM 02 · RECIPES

Give players the tools to make informed decisions

The recipe system supports the player's decisions by providing information about available dishes, their ingredients and how they should be prepared.

My intent

Give players a reference point when deciding what to cook, while connecting their choices to the cooking and grading systems.

Player-facing behaviour

Players consult a recipe book containing the available dishes and their preparation requirements.

What makes up a recipe?

  • 01 / BASE
    Base ingredient

    The main ingredient used as the foundation of the dish.

  • 02 / SIDES
    Additional ingredients

    One or more ingredients that complete the dish.

  • 03 / PREPARATION
    Cooking instructions

    Information about how the ingredients should be prepared.

Connection to grading

The recipes establish the requirements that are used to evaluate the player's finished dish.

Design flexibility

The number of available dishes and sides could be adjusted to change the complexity of deciding what to cook, creating opportunities for difficulty progression.

SYSTEM 03 · COOKING

Turning decisions into actions

Cooking forms the central activity of Squeak N' Serve. Players physically navigate between cooking stations, prepare ingredients and combine them to make dishes.

My intent

Make cooking an active process that encourages players to plan their preparation and navigate between stations, rather than simply selecting finished dishes from a menu.

Player-facing behaviour

The player carries ingredients to cooking stations, processes them using different interactions and combines ingredients according to their chosen recipe.

Three cooking stations

01 / BOILING
Boiling station

The player throws an ingredient into the station and presses a button to begin boiling.

02 / CUTTING
Cutting station

The player throws an ingredient into the station and repeatedly presses a button to cut it.

03 / FRYING
Frying station

Uses the same basic interaction as boiling, providing another preparation method for ingredients.

Combining ingredients

Ingredients can be combined by throwing one on top of another at a cooking station, allowing players to assemble dishes from multiple components.

SYSTEM 04 · GRADING

Rewarding good decisions, not just speed

The grading system evaluates the dish the player prepares, providing feedback based on the ingredients used, how they were cooked and the customer's taste.

My intent

Reward players for preparing the correct ingredients without making mistakes overly punishing. I wanted players to experiment and improve their cooking through experience.

Player-facing behaviour

After preparing a dish, the player throws it back to the customer. The game then evaluates the dish and provides a grade.

Simplifying an overengineered system

My initial design evaluated individual categories of a dish by increasing or decreasing a numerical score based on the player's performance.

On reflection, I considered this approach unnecessarily complicated for the experience I wanted to create. I replaced it with simpler grading tiers that communicated performance more directly.

01 / INITIAL APPROACH
Numerical scoring

Individual aspects of the dish contributed positive or negative values to a final score.

02 / REVISED APPROACH
Performance tiers

The grade communicates which requirements of the dish the player successfully fulfilled.

Design takeaway

A more complex system is not necessarily a better one. Simplifying the grading rules allowed me to preserve the intended reward structure without unnecessary complexity.

04 / DESIGN ITERATION

Exploring ways to deepen the cooking experience

Playtesting revealed opportunities to introduce more challenge, variation and reduce downtime. In response, I explored additional mechanics that could make cooking more engaging without relying primarily on conventional time limits.

The following concepts remained in the design phase and were not implemented in the playable game.

CONCEPT 01 · NOT IMPLEMENTED

Timing-based cutting

The existing cutting station relied on repeatedly pressing a button. I explored replacing this with a timing challenge where players would need to press a button when an indicator entered a target zone.

The goal was to introduce a skill-based interaction that required more precision from the player.

CONCEPT 02 · NOT IMPLEMENTED

Food temperature

I explored the idea of a temperature system where ingredients would heat up while boiling or frying and gradually cool down when no longer being cooked.

Recipes could require certain temperatures to achieve a Perfect grade, encouraging players to consider the order in which they prepared different ingredients.

CONCEPT 03 · NOT IMPLEMENTED

Seasoning

Another proposed system introduced three taste properties: sweetness, heat and saltiness, each measured on a scale from 1 to 5.

Players could season ingredients and taste their food up to two times safely. Recipes and customer preferences would establish the desired taste values.

The intention was to add another layer of precision and experimentation to preparing the ideal dish.

05 / PLAYER ONBOARDING

Teaching players through interaction

Early playtesting revealed that some players felt lost and were unsure of what they were supposed to do.

To address this, I designed a guided tutorial that introduced the cooking mechanics through practical actions rather than relying entirely on written explanations.

The Onboarding Challenge

How can players learn several interconnected cooking mechanics without becoming overwhelmed by instructions?

My approach

01 / LEARNING BY DOING

Introduce mechanics through actions

Players follow practical instructions and learn by performing the interactions themselves.

02 / VISUAL GUIDANCE

Show players where to go

A large arrow above the player points towards their next destination, reducing uncertainty about where to move.

03 / STEP-BY-STEP INSTRUCTIONS

Introduce one task at a time

The tutorial guides players through individual tasks, using icons instead of text whenever possible.

Feedback and tradeoffs

Later feedback suggested that the tutorial communicated its instructions clearly. Players appreciated the highlighting of essential words, and did not report any unclear tasks.

However, opinions about the amount of guidance were mixed. Some players found the tutorial too hand-holding, while others felt the guidance was appropriate.

This highlighted the challenge of balancing clarity with player independence.

06 / PLAYTESTING

What we learned from players

We conducted playtests to evaluate the game's visual style, cooking gameplay loop and tutorial. The feedback helped us identify both strengths and areas that needed further development.

WHAT WORKED

Players appreciated the core experience

Players generally responded positively to the cooking gameplay loop and felt that the grimy visual style suited a game about a rat running a food truck.

WHAT NEEDED IMPROVEMENT

More challenge and less downtime

Despite the positive reception, players identified opportunities to improve the cooking experience.

  • Reduce downtime between meaningful actions.
  • Introduce more challenge to cooking.
  • Increase variation in gameplay.
MY DESIGN RESPONSE

Exploring additional cooking mechanics

The feedback motivated me to explore new ways of introducing challenge and variation. I developed concepts for a timing-based cutting minigame, food temperature and seasoning.

These proposals were not implemented in the playable version, but helped me explore how the cooking loop could be expanded without abandoning the original design vision.

08 / REFLECTIONS

What I learned

Systems Design

Designing the cooking loop taught me how interconnected systems can support a shared gameplay goal. Ordering, recipes, cooking and grading needed to work together to create meaningful player decisions.

Player Onboarding

Designing my first game tutorial showed me the importance of teaching through interaction and giving players clear visual guidance. It also highlighted the challenge of balancing handholding with freedom.

3D Art & VFX

I gained experience creating visual effects in Unity and producing 3D assets within an established art style. The project also reinforced how visual presentation can contribute to gameplay communication.
